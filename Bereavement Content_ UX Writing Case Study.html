Writing for people at their hardest moment

How I wrote and structured a bereavement resource for a global bank, so people dealing with loss could find what to do next without feeling like they were dealing with paperwork.

RoleUX Writer
TeamProject manager and UX designers
MarketUnited Kingdom
ScopeUX research, copywriting, content style guide adherence, design collaboration

The challenge

The bank wanted a new bereavement section on its website and app. It had two jobs: tell customers what they need to do when someone dies, and give them a sense of support during a difficult time.

That is a hard writing brief. People arrive stressed, grieving and often unfamiliar with banking processes. The page had to be complete enough to be a real resource, but gentle enough not to add to the weight. It also had to sound like the bank, within a brand voice that is normally conversational and has a touch of wit.

Starting from the brand voice

The bank's tone of voice rests on four principles: human, insightful, confident and intelligent wit. Customers should be able to recognise the bank by how it writes, just as they would by its logo.

The final principle needed care. Wit has no place in a message about loss, and I wasn't going to force it. I leaned on the other three: human warmth, insight into what people actually need, and confidence in guiding them step by step. Following the style guide on a sensitive topic meant knowing which parts of the voice to turn up and which to put away.

PrincipleWhat it meant on this page
HumanAcknowledge the situation first. Write as a person who understands, not an institution processing a request.
InsightfulAnticipate what people will need next and answer it before they have to ask.
ConfidentTell people exactly what to do and how we'll help, in clear and direct terms.
Intelligent witSet aside for this context. Sensitivity takes priority over personality.

Defining the structure with the project manager

I worked with the project manager on the information architecture before drafting. The question was how to organise a large amount of information so that people who are overwhelmed could find their place quickly.

  • One entry point. A clear opening that acknowledges the situation and tells people support is available.
  • A step-by-step guide. The process broken into five stages, in the order people are likely to meet them.
  • Supporting pages. Detail on what to do when someone dies, FAQs and documents, and financial and emotional wellbeing, so the main page stays light.
Your step-by-step guide
Step 1 · Registering the deathWhen someone passes away, the death must be registered.
Step 2 · Letting us knowTell us, and find out which accounts they held with us.
Step 3 · Emergency needsWe can release funds from the estate for urgent costs.
Step 4 · Handling an estateThis can be complex, depending on each case.

Break it into steps

A long, formal process becomes five short stages. Each card gives one sentence on what the step is, so people can see the whole journey at a glance and know where they are. This is what makes the process feel manageable.

The writing decisions

Bereavement help

The death of someone close is always a difficult time.

Dealing with practical matters and making financial decisions can seem overwhelming. We're here to support you throughout.

Letting us know

We have a dedicated bereavement team to support you.

Notify us now

1. Lead with empathy, then with action

The first thing someone wants to deal with is not banking formalities. So the page opens by acknowledging that this can feel overwhelming, and says plainly that we're here to help. Only then does it move into what to do. The tone is reassuring but never sugary, and the main call to action is easy to find.

Important steps
What to do when someone diesYour first step is to register the death.
Financial and emotional wellbeingThere's a lot to think about. We have information to help.
FAQs and important documentsAnswers to common questions and a glossary of terms.

2. Anticipate needs and say what to do

People are already stressed, so the copy covers their questions before they ask them and says exactly what to do and how we can help. I listed the important steps in easy-to-consume pieces, kept the labels in plain language, and added a glossary so unfamiliar terms never become a barrier.

Financial and emotional wellbeing

Coping with bereavement can be challenging, and dealing with money isn't always straightforward.

Support we can offer
Financial fitnessFeel confident about your finances.
Money worriesWe can help you find the right solution.

3. Cover the whole picture

Some of what people need is unrelated to banking, such as arranging a funeral, locating a will or telling other organisations. I included it anyway. A page that only answered the bank's questions would leave people searching elsewhere at the worst time. Including this made the page a genuine resource, and showed support beyond accounts.

Working within constraints

This project had firm limits. The content had to follow the bank's internal style guide, sit within the established brand voice, and stay accurate on legal and financial processes, where careless wording could mislead someone. I worked closely with the designers so that the layout supported the copy, with short blocks, clear headings and generous spacing, rather than leaving the words to carry everything.

Outcome

The result was a single, structured bereavement resource across the website and app. It explains the process in five clear steps, surfaces the notification route prominently, and offers financial and emotional support alongside the practical guidance.

The page reassures people that they won't be doing this alone, and gives them everything they need to start.

What I learned

  • Brand voice is a range, not a fixed setting. Knowing what to dial down matters as much as knowing what to dial up.
  • When readers are stressed, structure is part of the tone. Short steps and clear labels are a form of kindness.
  • Completeness builds trust. Including information beyond the bank's own remit made the page more useful, and more human.
  • Next, I'd want to test the page with people who have been through a bereavement, to refine wording and ordering based on what they actually needed first.
