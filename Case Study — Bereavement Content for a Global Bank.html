One

A page that had to inform and comfort at the same time

The bank wanted a new bereavement section on its website. It had two jobs: tell customers what they need to do when someone dies, and give them a sense of support during a difficult time.

That is a hard writing brief. People arrive stressed, grieving and often unfamiliar with banking processes. The page had to be complete enough to be a real resource, but gentle enough not to add to the weight. It also had to sound like the bank, within a brand voice that is normally conversational and has a touch of wit.

Two

My role: structure, voice and clarity

I worked with the project manager and UX designers across three areas.

  • Information architecture — deciding how a large amount of information could be organised for people who are overwhelmed.
  • Copywriting — drafting the content to the bank's internal style guide and tone of voice.
  • Design collaboration — working with designers so the layout supported the copy, with short blocks, clear headings and generous spacing.
Three

Starting from the brand voice

The bank's tone of voice rests on four principles. Customers should recognise the bank by how it writes, just as they would by its logo.

HumanAcknowledge the situation first. Write as a person who understands, not an institution processing a request.
InsightfulAnticipate what people will need next and answer it before they have to ask.
ConfidentTell people exactly what to do and how we'll help, in clear and direct terms.
Intelligent witSet aside for this context. Sensitivity takes priority over personality.

Following a style guide on a sensitive topic means knowing which parts of the voice to turn up, and which to put away.

Five

The writing decisions

Four decisions shaped the page. Each followed the same pattern: what the reader needs, then how the copy responds.

A handful of things need to be done soon after someone has died. Start here and we'll guide you through each one.

1. Break the process into steps

A long, formal process becomes five short stages. Each card gives one sentence on what the step is, so people can see the whole journey at a glance and know where they are. That is what makes it feel manageable.

2. Lead with empathy, then action

The last thing someone wants to deal with is banking formalities. So the page opens by acknowledging that this can feel overwhelming, and says plainly that we're here to help. Only then does it move into what to do, with the main action easy to find.

3. Anticipate needs, tell people what to do

People are already stressed, so the copy covers their questions before they ask and says exactly what to do and how we can help. Labels are plain, steps are broken into easy-to-consume pieces, and a glossary means unfamiliar terms never become a barrier.

4. Cover the whole picture

Some of what people need is unrelated to banking, such as arranging a funeral, locating a will or telling other organisations. I included it anyway. A page that only answered the bank's questions would leave people searching elsewhere at the worst time.

Eight

What I'd carry forward

Three things I'd repeat on the next sensitive-content project.

  • Treat brand voice as a range, not a fixed setting. Knowing what to dial down matters as much as knowing what to dial up.
  • When readers are stressed, structure is part of the tone. Short steps and clear labels are a form of kindness.
  • Completeness builds trust. Including information beyond the bank's own remit made the page more useful, and more human.

Next, I'd test the page with people who have been through a bereavement, to refine wording and order around what they needed first.
